Onto-AIoTA Architecture Evaluation

Evaluating the five-layer Ontology-based IoT AI Architecture against DSR metrics of Validity, Utility, Efficiency, and Quality. Grounded in Requirements R1–R4 and Research Themes T1–T7.

Category 1
Validity — Does the architecture accurately represent and solve the real-world problem?
CQ-A1 How well does the architecture integrate ontology, IoT, and AI components into a coherent system?

Onto-AIoTA achieves strong integration across all three dimensions the name encodes — Ontology (Onto-), AI reasoning, and IoT data (AIoT) — through a clearly delineated five-layer architecture where each layer has a defined and non-redundant responsibility.

The IoT dimension is addressed at the Device Layer and Network Layer: collar-mounted sensors (GPS, accelerometer, temperature) generate raw telemetry, and LoRaWAN provides the low-power long-range communication channel that transmits it to the cloud. The Ontology dimension is operationalised in the Semantic Layer through the LFTDO Triple Store and the Data Mapper middleware, which transforms JSON/MQTT payloads into semantically typed RDF triples that the ontology can reason over. The AI dimension is realised by the Reasoning Engine (SWRL), which executes 27 classification rules against the live ABox to infer TheftEvents, EscapeEvents, and HealthAlerts in real time.

The integration is not merely additive — the three dimensions are causally chained: IoT data cannot become actionable intelligence without the ontology providing a shared typed vocabulary, and the ontology cannot generate alerts without the AI reasoning engine firing SWRL rules against live ABox instances. This causal dependency, made explicit in the four-phase semantic chain (Observation → Detection → Notification → Validation), is the architecture's core coherence argument.

One area warranting attention: the Application Layer requirement mapping shows no formal requirement assignment ("---"), which creates ambiguity about which non-functional properties (security protocols, latency SLAs, API versioning) govern user-facing components. Assigning it explicitly to R3 or R4 would complete the requirements coverage.

Strong Integration Across All Three Dimensions
CQ-A2 How practical is the architecture for real-world livestock farming scenarios in South Africa?

The architecture demonstrates deliberate and well-justified practical alignment with the South African agricultural context across three key dimensions.

Rural infrastructure: The selection of LoRaWAN as the primary network protocol directly addresses the connectivity gap documented in T3 (Rural Infrastructure Constraints). LoRaWAN's high link budget and low power consumption make it viable for farms without reliable cellular coverage. The solar-powered Device Layer tags address the off-grid power constraint, enabling continuous operation without grid dependency — critical for the remote Gauteng and Limpopo farming contexts where livestock theft is concentrated.

Crime intelligence alignment: The Business Layer's translation of TheftEvent classifications into governance-aligned actions maps directly to the operational reality described in T1 (Livestock Theft Crime Analysis). The notifies(LawEnforcementAgent) object property in LFTDO ensures that alerts reach the correct response chain, not only the farmer. This is a materially important design decision for a South African context where police response coordination is a recognised challenge.

POPIA compliance: The Business Layer includes explicit POPIA (Protection of Personal Information Act) compliance controls, which is a mandatory requirement for any South African research system handling farm geolocation and participant identity data. This demonstrates jurisdiction-specific design awareness beyond generic IoT architecture patterns.

High Practical Alignment with South African Context
Category 2
Utility (Usage) — How usable and adoptable is the architecture?
CQ-A3 Is the architecture suitable for rural deployment contexts given power and connectivity constraints?

The architecture is well-suited for rural deployment at the design level, with two of its five layers (Device and Network) explicitly mapped to R2 (Rural Power Constraint) and engineered around off-grid operation. The solar-powered device tag design and LoRaWAN protocol selection address the two primary rural infrastructure barriers — power and connectivity — in a technically coherent way.

LoRaWAN's characteristics are particularly relevant: typical LoRaWAN gateways cover 2–5 km in rural open terrain, which aligns with the farm-scale geofencing scenarios described in the LFTDO ABox. A single gateway can serve as the uplink for hundreds of collar-mounted sensors operating at low data rates (sensor telemetry is low-volume by nature), making the per-animal cost of connectivity low.

The architecture would benefit from an explicit fallback strategy for LoRaWAN outage scenarios — for example, when a gateway fails (Scenario 14 in the test suite). The current design notes that the Semantic Layer switches to historical data in this case, which is a valid degraded-mode strategy, but it is not formally documented as a layer-level requirement or component. Including a local edge caching mechanism at the gateway level would strengthen the rural resilience case.

Suitable with Minor Resilience Gap
CQ-A4 What are the potential barriers to architecture adoption by South African smallholder farmers?

Four adoption barriers are identifiable from the architecture design and the socio-technical framing of T2 and T6:

1. Technical literacy: The Semantic Layer introduces OWL 2, RDF triples, and SWRL concepts that are invisible to end users but require technical capacity to deploy, maintain, and troubleshoot. Smallholder farmers interacting with the Application Layer dashboard will not need to understand these, but any farm-level IT support or extension officer would. This creates a dependency on intermediary technical capacity that is currently unaddressed in the architecture's Business Layer governance model.

2. Device cost and theft of the devices themselves: Collar-mounted IoT tags represent a per-animal capital outlay. For smallholder farmers managing 10–50 animals, this cost must be weighed against the economic loss from theft. The architecture does not include a cost model or tiered deployment strategy (e.g., tagging only high-value animals first).

3. Trust and false alarm fatigue: The human-in-the-loop validation loop (validateStatusOfEvent) requires farmers to actively accept or reject TheftEvent classifications. If the False Alarm Rate is high in field conditions, alert fatigue will lead to disengagement — the "notification fatigue" problem well-documented in precision agriculture literature. The architecture addresses this technically (temporal duration thresholds, contextual disambiguation), but farmer training on interpreting alert confidence levels is not represented as a Business Layer component.

4. Network infrastructure dependency: While LoRaWAN is well-suited for rural use, farmers still require at least one gateway installation per farm. In areas where LoRaWAN network coverage is not yet available, the satellite fallback in the Network Layer would incur significantly higher costs.

Four Identifiable Barriers — Mitigation Strategies Recommended
Category 3
Efficiency — Does the architecture perform its tasks effectively?
CQ-A5 Does the architecture successfully perform its primary tasks (data ingestion, real-time inference, alert generation)?

At the conceptual design level, the architecture's task pipeline is logically sound and complete. The three primary tasks map cleanly onto the layer structure: data ingestion is handled by the Device Layer (sensor acquisition) and Network Layer (LoRaWAN transmission); real-time inference is the responsibility of the Semantic Layer's Reasoning Engine (SWRL rules executed by Pellet against live ABox triples); and alert generation is the output of the Application Layer, triggered by TheftEvent classification from the Semantic Layer via the SoftwareApplication notifies(Participant) object property.

The Pellet reasoner's documented performance of 659 ms for a consistency check at 57 individuals confirms that inference latency is acceptable for near-real-time detection at small ABox scales. However, the middleware accumulator design — which is required to pre-compute the hasDuration property before SWRL rules fire — introduces a deliberate latency by design (e.g., a 300-second duration threshold for RL_THEFT_IN_PROGRESS). This is the correct architectural trade-off: reducing false alarms is more operationally valuable than instantaneous alerting for sustained theft events. The architecture should document this latency-vs-precision trade-off explicitly in its Application Layer requirements.

Task Pipeline Logically Sound and Complete
CQ-A6 Does the architecture effectively bridge the gap between theoretical design and practical application?

The architecture bridges theory to practice through three concrete design decisions that go beyond abstract patterns. First, the LFTDO is not a generic IoT ontology — it is a domain-specific OWL 2 specification built from the South African livestock theft literature (T1, T4), with 105 classes and 27 SWRL rules calibrated against empirically observed livestock behaviour baselines (Dwyer, 2008). This grounding in domain literature is the key differentiator from generic architectural proposals. Second, the ABox population strategy using Cellfie CSV import enables practical data entry by domain experts without ontology engineering expertise, bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge representation and operational data management. Third, the human-in-the-loop validation loop (validateStatusOfEvent) explicitly acknowledges that automated systems cannot replace farmer judgement — particularly in South African contexts where animal movement is governed by local knowledge about grazing patterns, seasonal migration, and community land use. This is a mature design decision that theory-heavy architectures often omit.

Theory-to-Practice Bridge Well Constructed
CQ-A7 How cost-effective is the proposed architecture for rural South African deployment?

The architecture makes deliberate cost-efficiency choices at every hardware and software layer. The Device Layer specifies low-cost solar-powered tags rather than commercial livestock monitoring solutions, which typically carry high per-device licensing costs. LoRaWAN is an unlicensed spectrum protocol — there are no per-message data charges, unlike cellular IoT alternatives (NB-IoT, LTE-M). The LFTDO was developed using Protege (free, open-source) and OWL 2 (W3C open standard), eliminating commercial ontology platform costs. The cloud hosting of the Application Layer follows a standard SaaS model that can be provisioned on low-cost commodity infrastructure.

The primary cost drivers that are not addressed at the architecture level are: (1) the per-animal cost of the IoT collar tag hardware, which remains a meaningful barrier for smallholder farmers with small herds; and (2) the gateway infrastructure cost for farms outside existing LoRaWAN network coverage areas. A community-shared gateway model, where multiple neighbouring farms share a single LoRaWAN gateway, would reduce per-farm infrastructure costs and is consistent with the communal farming practices referenced in T2 (Socio-Technical Systems).

Cost-Effective Design — Hardware and Gateway Costs Require Modelling
CQ-A8 How flexible and adaptable is the architecture for new livestock species, sensors, or geographic regions?

The architecture demonstrates strong extensibility by design. The LFTDO's Cellfie-based ABox population mechanism allows new species to be added by creating new individual datasets in CSV format — no ontology re-engineering is required if the new species shares the same behavioural measurement schema (speed, temperature, GPS). The three-species ABox (Sheep, Cattle, Goat) already demonstrates this pattern: each species has its own baseline values (hasBaselineValues, hasDeviationThresholds) but shares the same TBox class hierarchy and SWRL rule structure.

Adding new sensor types (e.g., a heart rate monitor or RFID reader) would require extending the Device class hierarchy and the SensorData data properties in the TBox — a well-contained change that does not affect the reasoning logic or Application Layer. New geographic regions would require updating the geofence boundary coordinates in the ABox instances and potentially recalibrating speed baselines for different terrain types, but the architectural approach remains unchanged. The Business Layer's owl:versionInfo-based ontology versioning mechanism supports this iterative evolution without service disruption.

High Extensibility — Species, Sensors and Regions All Accommodated
Category 4
Quality — What is the design quality and innovation level of the architecture?
CQ-A9 How innovative is the Onto-AIoTA approach compared to existing livestock monitoring architectures?

Onto-AIoTA's primary innovation is the integration of a domain-specific OWL 2 ontology as the semantic middleware layer within an IoT-AI stack — a pattern that addresses a well-documented gap in existing livestock monitoring systems, which typically rely on threshold-based rule engines without formal knowledge representation. Existing systems such as GPS-based livestock trackers (e.g., Gallagher SmartFence, Ceres Tag) detect boundary breaches but cannot contextualise them: they cannot distinguish between a theft event, a health-related movement anomaly, an environmental stress response, or a device malfunction. The LFTDO's SWRL rule set — particularly the contextual disambiguation rules (RL_EXTREME_HEAT_CONTEXT, RL_COMPLEXTHEFT_CORRELATION, healthInfluencesBehaviourState) — is a direct architectural response to this limitation.

The middleware accumulator pattern for temporal reasoning is a further original contribution: standard SWRL is stateless and cannot reason about the duration of a state, which is the primary false-alarm source in movement-based detection. The hasDuration property managed by the middleware converts a known ontology limitation into a practical engineering solution. This is dissertation-worthy as a standalone contribution, as acknowledged in the Chapter 7 Supervisor's recommendations.

The five-layer naming convention (Onto-AIoTA) is also conceptually precise: the "Onto-" prefix signals that the semantic layer is not an add-on but the architectural core, which is an accurate characterisation of the design's dependency structure — every layer above the Semantic Layer depends on LFTDO classifications, and every layer below it exists to feed data into it.

Genuinely Novel Contribution — Semantic Middleware and Temporal Reasoning

The supplementary use of the Venable et al. (2016) FED framework to structure formative and summative evaluation phases adds methodological rigour to the evaluation design.

LFTDO Ontology Evaluation

Evaluating the Livestock Forensic Theft Detection Ontology against DSR metrics of Validity, Quality, Utility, and Efficacy. Grounded in the verified Protege structure (19,716 axioms · 105 classes · 27 SWRL rules · 1,865 individuals · TOQS 100%).

Category 1
Validity — Does the ontology accurately represent the livestock theft domain?
CQ-L1 Based on the verified ontology structure (19,716 axioms, 105 classes, 108 object properties, 172 data properties, 1,865 individuals), how consistent and logically sound is the LFTDO model as verified by the Pellet reasoner?

The verified ontology metrics indicate a structurally substantial and logically consistent OWL 2 DL knowledge base. The ratio of logical axioms (14,897) to total axioms (19,716) — approximately 75.5% — is typical of a well-populated ontology where the majority of content is definitional rather than annotational, indicating that the knowledge model is semantically dense rather than documentation-heavy.

The Pellet consistency check completing at 659 ms for 57 individuals (initial scale) confirms OWL-DL tractability at prototype scale. Pellet's selection over HermiT is justified by the performance differential (659 ms vs. 422,939 ms), and the G1GC JVM configuration demonstrates awareness of garbage collection behaviour under large ABox workloads.

The 4,577 ObjectPropertyAssertions against 1,865 individuals yields approximately 2.45 object property assertions per individual — a healthy density indicating that each livestock instance is meaningfully connected to multiple domain classes (Device, SensorData, BehaviourState, Location, Farmer) rather than being sparsely populated. The 7,194 DataPropertyAssertions (approximately 3.86 per individual) confirm that quantitative sensor readings are well-represented at instance level, which is the essential foundation for SWRL rule firing.

No logical inconsistencies were detected by the Pellet reasoner across the full ABox — a strong validity indicator confirming that the 27 SWRL rules do not produce contradictory classifications when applied to the 300-instance test population.

Logically Consistent — OWL-DL Tractability Confirmed at 300-Instance Scale
CQ-L2 Reviewing the 27 SWRL rules and the semantic chains in C2.4, how well does the ontology's RBox support real-time reasoning and inference across all scenario types?

The 27-rule RBox covers six detection categories (Theft, Health, Context, Escape, Normal, Security) and demonstrates strong coverage across all five scenario types in C2.4.

For Behavioural theft detection (Scenario 1 — Goat001): the three-rule combination RL_GOAT_ANOMALY_TRANSITION + RL_BEHAVIOUR_DEVIATION + RL_FENCE_BREACH_THEFT forms a coherent species-specific detection chain. The species-level rule (RL_GOAT_ANOMALY_TRANSITION) alongside the generic deviation rule (RL_BEHAVIOUR_DEVIATION) avoids over-generalisation while maintaining cross-species coverage — a sound ontological design decision.

For Multi-factor complex theft (Scenario 2 — Cow001): RL_COMPLEXTHEFT_CORRELATION is the most architecturally sophisticated rule, correlating three independently classified indicators (behavioural anomaly, health state, environmental context) into a single high-confidence TheftEvent. This three-indicator fusion is the key mechanism for reducing false positives while maintaining sensitivity, and its presence in the RBox is the primary technical differentiator from threshold-only detection systems.

For Health and resting scenarios (Scenario 3): the three prolonged resting rules (Intensity, Posture, StepCount) capture three distinct physiological dimensions of abnormal rest, preventing the health detection logic from being bypassed if a single sensor stream is noisy. This redundancy-by-design is appropriate for a real-world IoT context where individual sensor readings can be unreliable.

For Sabotage and system integrity (Scenario 4): RL_DEVICE_SABOTAGE_CHECK and RL_SYSTEM_INTEGRITY_CHECK address the adversarial context of livestock theft — where thieves may deliberately disable tracking devices — which is absent from most IoT livestock monitoring systems and represents a domain-specific originality contribution.

For Temporal validation (Scenario 5): the hasDuration middleware accumulator pattern correctly gates SWRL rule firing, producing the expected three-way classification (NormalActivity / SuspiciousActivity / TheftEvent) across the 100-sheep ABox partitioned by speed and duration conditions.

Full Scenario Coverage — Sophisticated Multi-Factor Correlation
CQ-L3 Do the seven SPARQL queries in C2.5 demonstrate that the LFTDO can answer all eight Competency Questions (CQ1–CQ8)?

The seven SPARQL queries in C2.5 cover CQ1, CQ2, CQ3, CQ4, CQ6, CQ7, and CQ8 directly. CQ5 (historical movement path of a livestock individual during a TheftEvent window) does not have a corresponding SPARQL query in C2.5 — this is the one competency question whose answer cannot be demonstrated from the current query set.

CQ5 requires temporal path tracking: a query that retrieves an ordered sequence of GPS coordinates associated with a specific livestock individual over a defined time window, filtered to a TheftEvent period. This query would require the SensorData instances to carry hasBehaviourStartTime and hasBehaviourEndTime properties (as demonstrated in the CQ4 query) combined with a time-window FILTER clause and ORDER BY hasTimeStamp. The TBox does include hasTimeStamp as a data property on SensorData, so the required data is present in the ABox — the gap is the absence of a query demonstrating its retrieval. This should be added to complete the CQ coverage.

The remaining seven queries are well-formed SPARQL SELECT statements that correctly reference LFTDO-namespaced properties. CQ3 is particularly notable: the FILTER(?isSensorDataAnomaly='true') clause demonstrates that the isSensorDataAnomaly data property is used as a Boolean flag in the ABox, enabling direct anomaly retrieval without requiring SWRL re-execution at query time.

CQ5 (Historical Movement Path) lacks a corresponding SPARQL query in C2.5. The data properties required (hasTimeStamp, GPS coordinates, TheftEvent time window) are all present in the TBox — a query should be added to complete competency question coverage.

CQ-L4 How well does the ontology represent the temporal and spatial dimensions of livestock movement and theft detection?

The LFTDO's spatial representation is comprehensive: four directional geofence rules (RL_GEOFENCE_NORTH_VIOLATION, RL_GEOFENCE_SOUTH, RL_GEOFENCE_WEST, RL_GEO_FENCE_MAXLON_EAST) cover all cardinal boundary directions, and RL_GEOFENCE_INSIDE_VALIDATION provides a positive confirmation of within-fence status. The RL_PROXIMITY_INFERENCE rule adds a pre-alert NearBoundary state that provides early warning before a breach occurs — this spatial gradient (Inside → NearBoundary → Outside) is a more nuanced representation than a binary geofence check and reflects realistic livestock movement patterns.

The temporal representation is the most architecturally original aspect of the design. Standard SWRL cannot reason over time sequences, and the middleware accumulator pattern — which manages hasDuration as a functional data property and only presents duration-confirmed assertions to the reasoner — is a principled solution to this limitation. The four temporal SWRL rules (RL_THEFT_DURATION, RL_NOCTURNAL_DURATION, RL_SIGNAL_JAMMING_DURATION, RL_GEOFENCE_SLOW_BREACH) cover the primary theft temporal patterns identified in the domain literature.

One area for future development: the ontology does not currently represent the concept of a livestock movement trajectory as a first-class TBox class. Individual GPS readings are captured as SensorData instances, but the aggregation of a sequence of readings into a Path or Route class — which would enable trajectory-based anomaly detection (e.g., path deviation from known grazing routes) — is not represented. This is noted as a future extension opportunity in the context of Scenario 5 (Path Deviation).

Strong Spatial Coverage — Temporal Reasoning Architecturally Original
Category 2
Quality — Does the ontology adhere to ontological best practices and engineering standards?
CQ-L5 How well does the LFTDO adhere to ontology engineering best practices and W3C standards?

The LFTDO demonstrates consistent adherence to ontology engineering best practices across naming, documentation, and formal compliance dimensions.

The naming convention — PascalCase for classes (Livestock, BehaviourState), camelCase for properties (isOutSideFence, hasVelocity), and species-prefixed PascalCase for individuals (Sheep001, SheepSensorData001) — follows established OWL ontology naming conventions and enables unambiguous distinction between the three ontology layers (TBox classes, RBox properties, ABox individuals) when reading ontology content.

Full annotation coverage (rdfs:label and rdfs:comment on every class, property, and individual — 2,570 AnnotationAssertions) exceeds the minimum documentation standard and enables human-readable inspection of the ontology without requiring ontology engineering expertise. This is a prerequisite for domain expert validation and future reuse.

OWL 2 DL compliance ensures that the ontology falls within a decidable description logic fragment, guaranteeing that the Pellet reasoner's completeness guarantee holds — all inferences that logically follow from the TBox and ABox will be computed. This is the correct choice for a safety-critical application where missed TheftEvent classifications (false negatives) have real-world consequences.

The six-stage Hybrid Methontology/OD-101 development methodology provides a traceable development audit trail from domain knowledge acquisition through to documentation, which is consistent with the best practice requirement for reproducible ontology engineering.

Comprehensive Best Practice Adherence — Full W3C OWL 2 DL Compliance
CQ-L6 The OOPS! evaluation returned TOQS = 100%. What does this result demonstrate, and what are its limitations as an evaluation method?

A TOQS of 100% — (13/13) × 100 — with no pitfalls detected across Critical, Important, and Minor categories confirms that the LFTDO is free from the 40 known structural ontology engineering pitfalls catalogued by the OOPS! scanner. Specifically: no class and property relationship errors (P05, P06, P07, P19, P24), no missing key concept representations (P04, P10, P11, P12, P13), and no terminology or definition issues (P03, P02, P21). This is a strong structural quality result and demonstrates engineering rigour in the ontology construction process.

However, OOPS! has well-documented scope limitations that are important to contextualise in a dissertation evaluation: the scanner evaluates structural and syntactic quality against a fixed pitfall catalogue. It does not evaluate: (1) semantic correctness — whether the class definitions and relationships accurately represent the livestock theft domain as understood by domain experts; (2) SWRL rule validity — whether the 27 rules correctly encode the intended reasoning logic without gaps or unintended interactions; (3) completeness relative to the domain — whether the 105 classes are sufficient to represent all relevant livestock theft scenarios; or (4) ABox instance quality — whether the 1,865 individuals correctly instantiate the TBox model.

For a complete quality evaluation, OOPS! results should be complemented by: expert review from livestock farming domain specialists, SWRL rule validation via the confusion matrix and scenario test suite (D.1–D.2), and competency question coverage testing via the SPARQL query set (C2.5).

TOQS 100% — Structural Quality Confirmed, Semantic Validity Requires Complementary Evaluation

Utility (Usage) — Is the ontology practically useful within the Onto-AIoTA ecosystem?
CQ-L8 How well does the LFTDO integrate with Onto-AIoTA Semantic Layer components (Data Mapper, Triple Store, Reasoning Engine)?

The LFTDO's integration with the Semantic Layer is architecturally tight and well-defined. The Data Mapper's responsibility — transforming JSON/MQTT sensor payloads into RDF triples typed against LFTDO classes — creates a direct coupling between the physical IoT device output and the ontology's ABox: each sensor reading becomes a SensorData individual, each device becomes a Device individual, and each livestock animal becomes a Livestock individual in the triple store. This coupling is made explicit in the four-phase semantic chain, where Phase 1 (Automated Observation) shows how raw sensor values become SWRL rule inputs through typed triple assertions.

The Triple Store functions as both the persistence layer for LFTDO ABox instances and the runtime workspace for the Reasoning Engine, which is the standard OWL reasoner architecture. The SPARQL endpoint exposed by the Triple Store provides the Application Layer with structured query access to inferred knowledge — TheftEvent classifications, BehaviourState transitions, and Participant notifications — without coupling the user interface to internal ontology structure.

The Reasoning Engine's use of the Pellet SWRL reasoner is well-matched to the LFTDO's OWL 2 DL expressivity: Pellet is the standard high-performance reasoner for this fragment and its SWRL support is required for the 27-rule RBox to execute. The integration is complete and the dependency chain is unambiguous.

Tight and Well-Defined Semantic Layer Integration
CQ-L9 How easy would it be to extend the LFTDO to include additional livestock species or new sensor types?

Extension is straightforward by design. For a new livestock species (e.g., horses): (1) create a new subclass of Livestock (e.g., Horse) with species-specific data property ranges for hasBaselineValues and hasDeviationThresholds — this is a TBox addition requiring Protege editing; (2) create a new species-specific behavioural deviation rule (e.g., RL_HORSE_ANOMALY_TRANSITION) modelled on RL_GOAT_ANOMALY_TRANSITION with horse-appropriate speed baselines (horses have significantly different flight speed profiles from sheep/goat/cattle); (3) populate the ABox via Cellfie by creating new CSV rows following the species-prefix convention (Horse001, HorseSensorData001, etc.). Steps 1 and 2 require an ontology engineer; Step 3 can be performed by a domain expert with a structured spreadsheet template.

For a new sensor type (e.g., heart rate monitor): create a new subclass of SensorData (e.g., HeartRateData) with a new data property (hasHeartRate, range xsd:float), add appropriate SWRL rules that reference the new property, and update the Data Mapper to transform the sensor's output format into the new triple pattern. This is a well-contained TBox extension that does not require modification of existing classes, properties, or rules.

Highly Extensible — Clear Pattern for Both Species and Sensor Extensions

Yes — the consistency of the semantic chain vocabulary across all five scenarios is a strong demonstration of the LFTDO's role as a shared lingua franca for the livestock theft detection domain. The recurring entities (StatusUpdateEvent, TheftIndicator, SoftwareApp, Farmer, Database) appear identically across Scenarios 1–5 regardless of the specific theft type, species, or detection mechanism involved. This consistency confirms that the TBox class hierarchy and object property definitions are stable and general enough to accommodate diverse scenario types without requiring scenario-specific modifications.

The stakeholder coverage in the Participant class hierarchy is comprehensive: Farmer (primary user), LawEnforcementAgent (response), Veterinarian (health follow-up), and ExtendedOfficer (farm management) each have distinct notification and validation roles encoded in the notifies and validateStatusOfEvent object properties. This multi-stakeholder vocabulary directly supports T2 (Socio-Technical Systems) by ensuring that the system's outputs are expressible in terms meaningful to each participant group, not only to the technical operators.

Full Scenario Coverage — Human-in-the-Loop Consistently Maintained
CQ-L12 How reliable and responsive is the LFTDO's alert and notification pipeline from TheftIndicator detection to Farmer notification?

The alert pipeline — TheftIndicator → initiates → TheftEvent → isDetectedBy → DetectionMethod → implements → SoftwareApplication → notifies → Participant — is a direct, linear object property chain with no ambiguous branch points. Each step has a single clearly defined object property in the TBox, ensuring that the pipeline is deterministic once a TheftIndicator is asserted by the SWRL reasoner.

The alert severity classification (High, Medium, Low) on TheftIndicator, combined with the TheftEvent status workflow (Pending → Accepted/Rejected), provides the operational triage information that a farmer or law enforcement agent needs to prioritise response without requiring them to interpret raw sensor data or ontology classes. This is the correct abstraction level for a user-facing notification system.

The separation of StatusUpdateEvent (farmer-facing confirmation event) from RoutineLogEvent (background logging) is an important reliability design decision: it prevents the audit trail from being polluted by user interaction events and ensures that the database record of TheftEvent classifications is independent of farmer response timing. This data integrity property is important for post-incident forensic investigation use cases (CQ5, CQ7).

CQ-L14 How significantly does the semantic context reduce false-alarm rates compared to non-semantic threshold-only detection systems?

The LFTDO's false-alarm reduction mechanisms operate at three distinct levels that a threshold-only system cannot replicate. The first is temporal gating: the hasDuration middleware accumulator prevents any speed-threshold breach from triggering TheftEvent classification until the breach has been sustained for at least 300 seconds. A threshold-only system firing on instantaneous speed alone would produce a False Positive for every animal startle event, play behaviour, or veterinary handling episode that exceeds the speed threshold briefly. In the 100-sheep temporal validation ABox (Scenario 5), the Sheep041–070 group (high speed, short duration) represents the direct measurement of this false-alarm reduction: every instance that a threshold-only system would incorrectly classify as TheftEvent is correctly classified as SuspiciousActivity by the LFTDO.

The second is contextual disambiguation: RL_EXTREME_HEAT_CONTEXT and RL_MODERATEHEAT_CONTEXT register ambient temperature as a contextual modifier, preventing high-activity states caused by heat stress from being misclassified as theft. RL_COMPLEXTHEFT_CORRELATION requires a conjunction of multiple indicator types (behavioural + health + environmental) before generating a high-confidence TheftEvent, which means no single-dimension anomaly can produce a false alarm at the highest severity level.

The third is health disambiguation: the healthInfluencesBehaviourState object property allows fever, injury, or lethargy to contextualise movement anomalies. A threshold-only system cannot distinguish between a sheep running at 5 m/s because it is being stolen and a sheep running at 5 m/s because a predator is inside the fence — the LFTDO's RL_COMPLEXTHEFT_CORRELATION can, by checking whether an inside-fence high-speed event is accompanied by spatial indicators that confirm outside-fence movement.

Collectively, these three mechanisms are expected to reduce the False Alarm Rate by a significant margin relative to the threshold-only baseline, particularly for the True Negative scenarios (Water Hole Clustering, Predator Attack Inside, Storm Response, Legitimate Relocation) that constitute the Group D false-alarm reduction test set.
